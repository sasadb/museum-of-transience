THE MUSEUM OF TRANSIENCE

The Museum of Transience collects what does not stay. Home, for me now, is made of things that do not last: Royco sachets that run out, train tickets good for one ride, twelve songs for the twelve months I could not go back. I keep rebuilding Indonesia in a small one-room, one meal at a time, and each time, it is a little different. For now, I keep those things here. Take your time.

ROOM I

Things That Carry Taste & Good Fortune

For ingredients, scents, and rituals that make an unfamiliar room feel like home.

OBJECT #01 • AI-RECONSTRUCTED PAINTING, 2026

Bamboe Soto Ayam

Soto Without the Warung

Back home, I almost never cooked soto. It was always nearby, warm and filling, from a roadside stall to a five-star hotel. In Korea, I have not found Indonesian restaurants selling it, and the seasoning is easy enough, so I had to make it myself. When late autumn arrives, it is what I cook again and again. The taste is not quite the same. There are no condiments, no fried snacks, no warung around the corner. Still, it brings something back.

OBJECT #02 • AI-RECONSTRUCTED PAINTING, 2026

Royco Sachet

The Measure of Everything

A sachet is small, but it holds a lot. Back home, it is just seasoned salt in a plastic pouch, something you buy in multiples without thinking. In Korea, when the supply gets low, I start measuring it carefully. Half a teaspoon for stir-fried greens, a pinch for fried eggs, a full spoonful when the soup tastes too flat and too far from home. It is the quickest way to make Korean produce taste like an Indonesian kitchen.

OBJECT #03 • AI-RECONSTRUCTED PAINTING, 2026

Desaku Bumbu Marinasi

Tempeh and the Yellow Salt

Back home, fried tempeh was everyday food. In Korea, tempeh is rare, and the seasoning packet is hard to come by. This yellow powder, mostly turmeric and coriander, is what turns plain soy into something that smells like a Sunday morning in Central Java. One packet lasts five or six meals if I am careful. When it is gone, I will have to mix coriander and turmeric myself, but it is never quite the same ratio.

OBJECT #04 • AI-RECONSTRUCTED PAINTING, 2026

Ladaku White Pepper

Heat That Stings First

Korean black pepper is fine and mild. Indonesian white pepper stings differently. It hits the back of the throat before it hits the tongue. Back home, every kitchen table has a bottle or a sachet with a small hole cut in the corner. Here, I keep two sachets in a plastic drawer, saving them for days when the weather drops below zero and soup needs to bite back.

OBJECT #05 • AI-RECONSTRUCTED PAINTING, 2026

Tong Tji Jasmine Tea Bag

Sweetness Before Sleeping

In Korea, tea is barley or corn or green. Jasmine tea—the heavy, sweet, dark brew from Solo and Semarang—is something you have to bring with you. I drink it hot, late at night, in a plastic mug that has seen too many uses. The scent of jasmine fills the small room, masking the smell of the radiator and the cold outside. For twenty minutes, until the cup goes cold, I am not in Daejeon.

OBJECT #06 • 3D SCAN, 2026

Crusita Es Teh Perfume

Uniquely Indonesian, Quietly So

It smells like iced sweet tea from a roadside stall on a hot afternoon—sugar, warm water, heavy jasmine, and a little bit of ice melting in a plastic bag. I bought it before leaving Indonesia, not knowing if I would actually wear it. In Korea, I spray it on my wrist before going to the library. It does not smell like any perfume sold here. People ask what it is, and I say it is tea, but I mean home.

ROOM II

Things That Record the Journey

For paper, plastic, and stubs that prove I was there, even for a moment.

OBJECT #07 • 3D SCAN, 2026

Bukeo Train Ticket

Standing Room Only

Bukeo means no assigned seat. You buy it when the regular tickets sell out, and you stand in the aisle between cars or sit on your bag near the door. This one is from a Friday evening trip from Seoul back to Daejeon. The train was full of students and workers going home for the weekend. I sat on my backpack for two hours, watching the dark countryside pass through the window gap, holding this small slip of thermal paper so the conductor could check it.

OBJECT #08 • INTERACTIVE ARCHIVE, 2026

The Ticket Collection Wall

Receipts of Being Elsewhere

Paper tickets in Korea are dying out; most people use an app on their phone. But when you buy at the counter or use a paper machine, you get these thin, narrow receipts that fade after three months. I kept all of them: Korail receipts, Intercity bus tickets, museum entry passes, concert stubs. Arranged together, they form a map of movements I have already forgotten making.

OBJECT #09 • 3D SCAN, 2026

Jeong Bokhyun's Namecard

Exchange at First Meeting

In Korea, receiving a namecard is a small ceremony: two hands, a bow, a quick glance at the name before putting it away respectfully. This card was given to me during my first month in Daejeon. I did not have my own card to offer in return—only a bow and an apology in broken Korean. I kept it on my desk as a reminder that I was now in a place where who you are is handed to you on thick paper, double-sided, with two languages.

ROOM III

Things I Replay

For sound, rhythm, and songs that stayed when everything else moved on.

OBJECT #10 • AUDIO ARCHIVE, 2026

Twelve Songs for Twelve Months

The Year in Sound

One song for each month away from home. Some are Indonesian indie tracks heard on rainy evenings in the dormitory; others are Korean songs learned from convenience store speakers and subway station announcements. Together, they form a timeline of moods—from the excitement of arriving in September to the quiet fatigue of February, and the slow acceptance of spring.

ROOM IV

Things I Carried With Me

For objects that survived the luggage weight limit and stayed on the desk.

OBJECT #11 • 3D SCAN, 2026

Custom Lego Minifigure

Small Companion

A gift from a friend before I left Jakarta. It sits on top of my computer monitor, looking down at the keyboard. It has survived three room moves and two semester finals. In a room where almost everything was provided by the university—the desk, the chair, the bed, the wardrobe—this tiny plastic person is one of the few things that came from my old life and has no function other than being there.

OBJECT #12 • 3D SCAN, 2026

Dormitory Aroma Diffuser

The Scent of a Temporary Room

Bought at a Daiso near the station during my second week. Dormitory rooms all have the same smell when you move in: cold wood, cleaning alcohol, and the faint trace of whoever lived there before you. It took three weeks of lavender oil to make the room smell like mine. Now, whenever I open the door after a long day at the library, this is what tells me I am back, at least for tonight.

Nothing here is permanent.
The sachets will be used, the tickets will fade, the perfume will run out.
This museum exists only while these things are still here.
